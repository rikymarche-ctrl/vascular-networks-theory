\section{The Retinal Paradox: Mechanical-Fluidic Decoupling}
\label{sec:retinal}

The human retina provides a unique test case for the incommensurability
principle. Unlike three-dimensional vascular beds (coronary, pulmonary), retinal
vessels are constrained to a quasi-two-dimensional manifold (the retinal
surface). According to the dual-threshold framework derived from the Kinematic
Matching Criterion (Theorem~\ref{thm:kinematic_matching},
Section~\ref{sec:womersley_minimax}), this dimensional constraint produces a
profound qualitative shift:

The ``paradox'' that gives this section its name is a mismatch in apparent
dimensionality \emph{within a single network}: the vessel \emph{diameters} obey
a two-dimensional, wave-matching law ($\alpha\approx 2.0$), while the
bifurcation \emph{angles} sit far from the planar $90^\circ$ that the same 2D
fluid argument would suggest, hugging instead a mechanical-tension optimum. No
single local optimization criterion can deliver both at once. The
incommensurability framework resolves the tension by recognizing that the two
features answer to \emph{different} channels: the diameters are fixed by the
fluid/wave balance, which is acutely sensitive to the embedding dimension and
collapses to the wave attractor in a plane (below), whereas the branching angles
are fixed by a local mechanical tension equilibrium that is largely blind to
that fluidic collapse. Diameters and angles are therefore
\emph{decoupled}---exactly the mechanical--fluidic decoupling of the section
title---and need not report the same effective dimension.

\subsection{Predicted Dimensional Scaling}

The fluid and wave thresholds scale differently with dimension $d$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
\textbf{Fluid threshold (local admittance $Y_L$):}
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}}(d) = \sqrt{6/(d-1)}$

For $d=3$: $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$

For $d=2$: $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{6} \approx \VarWoCTwo$

    \item
\textbf{Wave threshold (characteristic admittance $Y_c \propto
\sqrt{i\omega\rho\,Y_L}$, equivalently $Y_c\propto\sqrt{1-F_{10}}$):}
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}}(d) = \sqrt{3d(d-2)/(d-1)}$

For $d=3$: $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} = 3/\sqrt{2} \approx 2.121$

For $d=2$: $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} = 0$ (collapses identically)
\end{itemize}

The collapse of $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}}$ to zero in planar topology
implies that \emph{any} finite Womersley number in 2D networks places the wave
permanently above its kinematic matching threshold ($\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_c} \ge
1$ for all $\mathrm{Wo} > 0$). This geometric constraint forces retinal vessels
into a permanent wave-dominated regime.

Because the human retinal vasculature is embedded in a systemic tree operating
well above the 2D transition mass ($M \gg M^*_{d=2}$), the dimensional
constraint shifts the minimax diameter attractor toward the area-preserving
limit. The dimensional prediction is therefore channel-specific:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
\textbf{Diameters:} $\alpha_{\text{retina}} \approx 2.0$ (area-preserving
wave limit).
    \item
\textbf{Angles:} the \emph{counterfactual} planar fluid rule would give
$\varphi \approx 90^\circ$, but the mechanical wall-tension channel remains
closer to the three-dimensional Murray equilibrium, predicting $\varphi \approx
\VarAngleRetinalCalcDeg^\circ$.
\end{itemize}

For comparison, Murray's law in 3D predicts $\alpha_{\mathrm{3D}} \approx 3.0$
(viscous) and $\varphi_{\mathrm{3D}} \approx \VarAngleTetrahedralCalcDeg^\circ$
(derived from the energy-minimization condition $\cos(\varphi/2) = 2^{-1/3}$ for
symmetric bifurcations).

\subsection{Observational Evidence}

\citet{luo2017} measured 342 bifurcations in healthy human retinal vessels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
\textbf{Diameters:} $\alpha_{\text{obs}} \approx 2.0$--$2.7$ (range across
vessel types; consistent with area-preserving limit)
    \item
\textbf{Angles:} $\varphi_{\text{obs}} = \VarAngleRetinalObs^\circ \pm
\VarAngleRetinalErr^\circ$ (SE), with standard deviation $\sigma =
\VarAngleRetinalSD^\circ$
\end{itemize}

The diameter scaling ($\alpha_{\text{obs}} \approx 2.0$--$2.7$) is consistent
with the area-preserving attractor predicted by the 2D dimensional constraint.
The observed mean angle $\VarAngleRetinalObs^\circ$ lies between the 3D Murray
prediction ($\VarAngleRetinalCalcDeg^\circ$) and the 2D planar limit
($90^\circ$), departing from the 3D Murray prediction by only
$\VarAngleRetinalOffset / \VarAngleRetinalSD \approx 0.20\sigma$ (whereas the
gap to the planar $90^\circ$ limit is $0.63\sigma$); however, given the large
biological variance ($\sigma = \VarAngleRetinalSD^\circ$), the angular data are
consistent with mechanical--fluidic decoupling but are not decisive by
themselves. The high variance likely reflects stochastic angiogenesis in vivo,
where local biochemical gradients (VEGF, shear stress) obscure the geometric
signal at individual junctions.

\subsection{Effective Viscous Limit and the Fåhræus-Lindqvist Effect}

An additional subtlety arises from the reduction of apparent blood viscosity in
narrow vessels---the Fåhræus-Lindqvist effect. Fåhræus and
Lindqvist~\cite{fahraeuslindqvist1931} showed that the apparent viscosity of
blood departs from Poiseuille's law in tubes below a diameter of $\sim 0.3$\,mm,
falling to roughly half of its large-tube value by a diameter of $\sim 30\,\mu$m
\emph{in vitro}. In the still narrower retinal microvessels this reduction
lowers the effective transport exponent below the classical Murray value
($\alpha = 3.0$) toward $\alpha_{\text{eff}} \approx \VarAlphaFahraeusEff$
(a conservative \emph{in-vivo} estimate, as apparent viscosity in living
microvessels is attenuated relative to the glass-tube measurements
of~\cite{fahraeuslindqvist1931}). This places retinal diameter scaling between
the wave attractor ($\alpha_w = 2.0$) and the corrected viscous limit
($\alpha_{\text{eff}} = \VarAlphaFahraeusEff$), consistent with the
transition-regime interpretation.

Taken together, the retinal data provide a case study in which the diameter
scaling supports the dimensional decoupling predicted by the
incommensurability framework, while the angular evidence remains inconclusive
due to biological variance.
