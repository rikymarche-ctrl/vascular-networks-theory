\section{Mathematical Derivation of the Impedance and Admittance Expansions}
\label{sec:S1}

In plain terms, the entire dual-threshold result hinges on how a vessel's
admittance behaves when pulsatility is weak (low Womersley number). This section
extracts that low-$\mathrm{Wo}$ behaviour explicitly, so that the critical
thresholds quoted in the main text emerge as simple algebraic conditions rather
than remaining buried in the full Bessel-function solution.

We derive the low-Womersley expansion of the longitudinal and characteristic
admittances used in the wave-cost calculation of the main text. The Womersley
function is $F_{10}(z) = 2J_1(z)/(zJ_0(z))$, with $z = \mathrm{Wo} e^{-i\pi/4}$.
Utilizing the series expansions for $J_0$ and $J_1$:
\begin{equation}
    J_0(z) \approx 1 - \frac{z^2}{4} + \frac{z^4}{64}, \quad J_1(z) \approx \frac{z}{2} - \frac{z^3}{16} + \frac{z^5}{384}.
\end{equation}
The Womersley function expands as:
\begin{equation}
    F_{10}(z) = \frac{2J_1(z)}{z J_0(z)} \approx 1 + \frac{z^2}{8} + \frac{z^4}{48} + \mathcal{O}(z^6).
\end{equation}
Given $z = e^{-i\pi/4}\,\mathrm{Wo}$ (where $i^{3/2} = e^{i3\pi/4}$, consistent
with the $e^{i\omega t}$ time convention), we have $z^2 = -i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2$.
The complex longitudinal admittance term $1 - F_{10}$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
    1 - F_{10} \approx -\frac{(-i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2)}{8} - \frac{(-i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2)^2}{48}
    = \frac{i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2}{8} + \frac{\mathrm{Wo}^4}{48}.
\end{equation}
The longitudinal fluid admittance $Y_L \propto (1 - F_{10})/(i\omega\rho)$
therefore yields a positive real part and a negative imaginary part. The
temporal partitioning of pulsatile energy for the bulk fluid is governed by its
inverse quality factor $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L}$, measuring the ratio of active
power dissipation (resistive) to reactive power storage (inductive):
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L} = \frac{\text{Re}(Y_L)}{|\text{Im}(Y_L)|} = \frac{1}{\mathrm{Wo}^2/6} = \frac{6}{\mathrm{Wo}^2}.
\end{equation}
Applying the kinematic matching condition $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L} = d - 1$
yields the critical fluid threshold $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} =
\sqrt{\frac{6}{d-1}}$. For $d=3$, this gives $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} =
\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$.

However, wave reflection in the network is governed by the characteristic
admittance $Y_c$. With the exact longitudinal definition $Y_L =
C(1-F_{10})/(i\omega\rho)$ for a positive real geometric constant $C$, one has
$i\omega\rho\,Y_L = C(1-F_{10})$; hence $Y_c\propto\sqrt{1-F_{10}}$ up to
positive real constants. Taking this square root algebraically halves the phase
angle $\phi = \arctan(6/\mathrm{Wo}^2)$. Using the half-angle identity
$\cot(\phi/2) = \frac{1+\cos\phi}{\sin\phi}$, and knowing from a right triangle
that $\tan\phi = 6/\mathrm{Wo}^2$ implies $\sin\phi =
\frac{6}{\sqrt{36+\mathrm{Wo}^4}}$ and $\cos\phi =
\frac{\mathrm{Wo}^2}{\sqrt{36+\mathrm{Wo}^4}}$, the wave's inverse quality
factor evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_c} = \cot\left(\frac{1}{2}\arctan\left(\frac{6}{\mathrm{Wo}^2}\right)\right) = \frac{\sqrt{36 + \mathrm{Wo}^4} + \mathrm{Wo}^2}{6}.
\end{equation}
Applying the same topological constraint $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_c} = d - 1$ to the
propagating wave yields:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\sqrt{36+\mathrm{Wo}^4}+\mathrm{Wo}^2}{6} = d - 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sqrt{36+\mathrm{Wo}^4} = 6(d-1) - \mathrm{Wo}^2.
\end{equation}
Squaring both sides and canceling the $\mathrm{Wo}^4$ terms gives:
\begin{equation}
    36 = 36(d-1)^2 - 12(d-1)\mathrm{Wo}^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 12(d-1)\mathrm{Wo}^2 = 36d(d-2).
\end{equation}
This isolates the theoretical wave threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} = \sqrt{\frac{3d(d-2)}{d-1}}.
\end{equation}
For $d=3$, this gives $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} = 3/\sqrt{2} \approx
2.121$. The exact numerical root of $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L} = 2$ from the full
Bessel solution is $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} \approx 1.740$, which is
within $0.5\%$ of the analytical second-order result $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$.

\section{Kinematic Matching and the Womersley Transition}
\label{sec:S2}

This section presents an alternative energetic derivation of the fluid threshold
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}}$ from first principles via energy equipartition
in pulsatile flow. This complements the complex admittance approach of
Section~\ref{sec:S1} and provides physical insight into the local
viscous-inertial balance governing the bulk fluid.

\subsection{Womersley Flow Profile}

\paragraph{Governing equation.}
Consider axisymmetric pulsatile flow in a rigid cylindrical vessel of radius
$R$. The velocity profile $v_z(r, t)$ satisfies the Navier-Stokes equation:
\begin{equation}
    \rho \frac{\partial v_z}{\partial t}
    = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial z}
    + \mu_f \left(\frac{\partial^2 v_z}{\partial r^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial r}\right),
\end{equation}
with no-slip boundary condition $v_z(r=R, t) = 0$.

For sinusoidal pressure gradient $-\partial p/\partial z = A \cos(\omega t)$, we
seek a solution $v_z(r, t) = \mathrm{Re}[\hat{v}(r) e^{i\omega t}]$, yielding
the Womersley equation:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d^2\hat{v}}{dr^2} + \frac{1}{r}\frac{d\hat{v}}{dr}
    - \frac{i\omega\rho}{\mu_f} \hat{v} = -\frac{A}{\mu_f}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Analytical solution.}
Define the Womersley number $\mathrm{Wo} = R\sqrt{\omega\rho/\mu_f}$ and
dimensionless coordinate $\xi = r/R$. The solution is:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{v}(\xi) = \frac{A}{i\omega\rho}
    \left[1 - \frac{J_0\left(z\xi\right)}{J_0\left(z\right)}\right],
    \quad z = \mathrm{Wo} e^{-i\pi/4},
\end{equation}
where $J_0$ is the Bessel function of the first kind, order zero.

\subsection{Energy Balance in Pulsatile Flow}

\paragraph{Kinetic energy per unit length.}
The cycle-averaged kinetic energy per unit axial length is:
\begin{equation}
    \langle E_{\mathrm{kin}} \rangle
    = \frac{\pi\rho R^2}{2} \int_0^1 |\hat{v}(\xi)|^2 \xi \, \mathrm{d}\xi.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Viscous dissipation rate and energy per radian.}
The viscous dissipation rate per unit length, averaged over a cardiac cycle, is:
\begin{equation}
    \langle P_{\mathrm{visc}} \rangle
    = \pi\mu_f \int_0^1 \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{v}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\right|^2 \xi \, \mathrm{d}\xi.
\end{equation}
To compare this rate with the kinetic energy $\langle E_{\mathrm{kin}} \rangle$
(which represents stored energy, not a rate), we define the viscous energy cost
per unit radian of the cardiac cycle, $\langle E_{\mathrm{visc}} \rangle =
\langle P_{\mathrm{visc}} \rangle / \omega$. This definition ensures strict
dimensional consistency in the energy ratio:
\begin{equation}
    \langle E_{\mathrm{visc}} \rangle
    = \frac{\pi\mu_f}{\omega} \int_0^1 \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{v}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\right|^2 \xi \, \mathrm{d}\xi.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Dimensionless energy ratio.}
The ratio of kinetic to viscous energies is:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\langle E_{\mathrm{kin}} \rangle}{\langle E_{\mathrm{visc}} \rangle}
    = \frac{\mathrm{Wo}^2}{2} \cdot \mathcal{K}(\mathrm{Wo}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{Wo})$ is a dimensionless function determined by
Bessel function integrals:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{K}(\mathrm{Wo}) = \frac{\int_0^1 \left|1 - \frac{J_0(z\xi)}{J_0(z)}\right|^2 \xi \, \mathrm{d}\xi}
    {\int_0^1 \left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}\left[\frac{J_0(z\xi)}{J_0(z)}\right]\right|^2 \xi \, \mathrm{d}\xi}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Geometric Bound and the Fluid Critical Threshold}

A crude kinetic-viscous equipartition estimate for a rigid-wall tube gives
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{eq}} \approx 2.35$; this coarse criterion does
\emph{not} coincide with the fluid threshold and is not used to justify it. The
operative threshold follows instead from the isotropic power-flow embedding of
the branching network derived in Section~\ref{sec:S4}, which gives
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^2 = 6/(d-1)$. At the physical embedding dimension $d=3$ this
reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Wo}_c^2 = \frac{6}{d-1} = 3 \quad (d=3)
    \quad \Rightarrow \quad
    \boxed{\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{3} \approx 1.732,}
\end{equation}
the coincidence $6/(d-1)=d$ being special to $d=3$. This geometric
threshold $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{3}$ governs the local
viscous-inertial balance of the bulk fluid, constraining the energetic
optimization of individual segments. As shown in Section~\ref{sec:S1}, applying
the same kinematic matching criterion to the characteristic wave admittance
$Y_c$, with $Y_c \propto \sqrt{i\omega\rho\,Y_L}$ and equivalently
$Y_c\propto\sqrt{1-F_{10}}$ under the longitudinal definition above, yields a
distinct wave threshold $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} = 3/\sqrt{2} \approx
2.121$. The fluid threshold $\sqrt{3}$ derived here via energy balance
corresponds to the longitudinal admittance $Y_L$ in the dual-threshold
framework.

\subsection{Physiological Validation}

For human parameters ($\rho = \VarRho\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\mu_f =
\VarMuFmPas\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$, $f_H =
\VarHeartRateHuman\,\mathrm{bpm}$)~\cite{huo2012}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Wo}_g = r_g \times \VarWoCoeffHuman\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.
\end{equation}
Setting $\mathrm{Wo}_g = \mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{3}$ gives the
critical radius:
\begin{equation}
    r_g^{\mathrm{crit}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\VarWoCoeffHuman} \approx \VarRadiusCritMm\,\mathrm{mm},
\end{equation}
corresponding to small conduit arteries (distal brachial, tibial). The hierarchy
spans:
\begin{align}
    \text{Aorta ($r_0 \approx 12$\,mm):}\ &\mathrm{Wo}_0\approx17.9\quad (\text{inertial}), \\
    \text{Large arteries ($r_3 \approx 4$\,mm):}\ &\mathrm{Wo}_3\approx6.0\quad (\text{inertial}), \\
    \text{Critical generation ($r \approx \VarRadiusCritMm$\,mm):}\ &\mathrm{Wo}\approx1.73\quad (\text{critical}), \\
    \text{Arterioles ($r_8 \approx 0.1$\,mm):}\ &\mathrm{Wo}_8\approx0.15\quad (\text{viscous}).
\end{align}
The network spans all three flow regimes; the critical condition is satisfied in
the distal conduit arteries. Furthermore, developmental alterations in vascular
load during embryonic growth demonstrate that the cardiovascular system actively
remodels to maintain the critical balance of pulsatile energy and characteristic
impedance near this transition zone~\cite{lucitti2005}.

\section{Decomposition of the Structural Residual}
\label{sec:S3}

The purpose of this section is to show that the small gap between the
symmetric-model exponent ($\alpha^*\approx\VarAlphaStarModel$) and the
large-mammal value ($\alpha^*\approx\VarAlphaStar$) is not an adjustable fudge
factor but the sum of two \emph{independently measured} structural
effects---bifurcation asymmetry and vessel taper---that push the exponent in
opposite directions. Decomposing the residual in this way makes each
contribution separately checkable against morphometry.

The symmetric minimax model predicts a scale-dependent minimax value $\alpha^*
\approx 2.60\text{--}2.68$ near the allometric transition (agreeing with rat
pulmonary arteries at $\alpha \approx 2.68$, Jiang et al.
(1994)~\cite{jiang1994}, and predicting $\alpha^* \approx \VarAlphaStarModel$
for humans at $70\,\mathrm{kg}$). Large mammals, operating far from the
transition threshold, exhibit systematically higher values ($\alpha^* \approx
\VarAlphaStar$ in porcine coronaries~\cite{kassab1993}). This $\Delta\alpha^*
\approx \VarAlphaResidual$ shift represents the accumulation of scale-dependent
morphometric heterogeneities.

To formally demonstrate that this residual arises from opposing, physically
coherent forces, we present a simplified first-order computational decomposition
(see the validation script
\texttt{scripts/verification/verify\_heterogeneity.py}) evaluating the two
primary structural deviations from the ideal model: \emph{bifurcation asymmetry}
and \emph{continuous vessel taper}.

\paragraph{The Effect of Asymmetry.}
In an ideal symmetric junction ($r_1 = r_2$), perfect impedance matching (zero
reflection) occurs exactly at $\alpha = 2$. When the junction is asymmetric ($A
= r_2/r_1 < 1$), the difference in vessel radii forces the two branches to
operate at distinct local Womersley numbers. This mismatch in viscous velocity
profiles breaks the perfect-matching condition, effectively shifting the minimum
of the wave-reflection penalty away from $\alpha=2$ toward higher values
($\alpha \approx 2.5$). Asymmetry shifts the local wave-reflection
attractor upward, while its net effect on the first-order minimax saddle is
small and slightly downward in the rigid-wall calculation.

\paragraph{The Downward Pull of Taper and Coherence.}
Conversely, continuous distal vessel tapering increases the characteristic
impedance along the segment, introducing a distributed reflection penalty. This
effect, along with constructive coherent wave interference, strictly
\emph{amplifies} the global magnitude of the wave penalty. To minimize an
increasingly severe wave reflection cost, the system is forced to retreat from
the transport optimum ($\alpha=3$) and shift toward the wave minimum. Thus,
taper and coherence pull the minimax equilibrium \emph{downward}.

\paragraph{Explicit Calculation: The Asymmetry Shift.}
To quantify the effect of asymmetry on the wave-reflection minimum, we
consider a single junction with parent radius $r_0$ and daughters $r_1, r_2$.
Define the asymmetry ratio $A = r_2/r_1 \le 1$ (daughter-to-daughter radius
ratio). The impedance-matching condition for a reflectionless junction is $Y_0 =
Y_1 + Y_2$. In the high-Womersley regime, $Y \propto r^2$. For $A < 1$, the
area-preservation condition $r_0^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2$ remains the geometric
baseline.

However, the viscous corrections to the wave speed (governed by
$F_{10}(\mathrm{Wo})$) differ for $r_1$ and $r_2$. The local Womersley number
scales as $\mathrm{Wo} \propto r$. Let $\mathrm{Wo}_1 = \mathrm{Wo}_0
(r_1/r_0)$. The reflection coefficient $\Gamma$ for a junction with $\alpha = 2$
is:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma = \frac{Y_0 - (Y_1 + Y_2)}{Y_0 + (Y_1 + Y_2)} \approx \frac{\Delta Y_0 - (\Delta Y_1 + \Delta Y_2)}{2 Y_0},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta Y$ represents the asymptotic complex viscous deviation $\propto
1/\mathrm{Wo}$. Substituting the scaling $r_g = r_0 2^{-g/\alpha}$, we find that
for $A < 1$, the minimum of $|\Gamma|^2$ shifts from $\alpha=2$ to a higher
value $\alpha_w(A)$. For the porcine coronary mean $A \approx
\VarHeteroAsymmetryA$, this local attractor shifts to $\alpha_w \approx 2.5$.

\paragraph{Continuous Taper.}
The presence of a continuous radius reduction (taper) along the vessel length
introduces a distributed reflection $\Gamma_{\mathrm{taper}} \approx -
\frac{1}{2} \int_0^L \frac{1}{Z} \frac{\mathrm{d}Z}{\mathrm{d}s} e^{-2iks} \,
\mathrm{d}s$. For a $\VarHeteroTaperPercent\%$ taper, this adds a persistent
\emph{braking} reflection that increases the wave cost. The total incoherent
reflection coefficient at each generation is computed by combining the junction
and taper reflections in series:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\mathrm{combined}} = \Gamma_{\mathrm{junction}} + (1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{junction}}) \Gamma_{\mathrm{taper}},
\end{equation}
which increases the overall wave cost $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wave}}$, forcing the
minimax $\alpha^*$ downward.

\paragraph{First-Order Numerical Estimation.}
Using empirical physiological parameters computed from the porcine coronary
morphometric data~\cite{kassab1993,kaimovitz2008}---a mean daughter asymmetry
ratio of $A \approx \VarHeteroAsymmetryA$ (derived from measured bifurcation
diameter distributions) and a continuous taper of approximately
$\VarHeteroTaperPercent\%$ radius reduction per generation (computed from
generation-to-generation diameter changes)---our first-order estimator yields
the following saddle-point shifts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item
\textbf{Base Symmetric Model}: $\alpha^* = \VarAlphaHeteroBase$
    \item
\textbf{Empirical Asymmetry} ($A=\VarHeteroAsymmetryA$): $\alpha^* =
\VarAlphaHeteroAsym$
    \item
\textbf{Empirical Taper} ($\VarHeteroTaperPercent\%$ distal reduction):
$\alpha^* = \VarAlphaHeteroTaper$
    \item
\textbf{Combined Asymmetry + Taper}: $\alpha^* = \VarAlphaHeteroCombined$
\end{itemize}
These values represent independently constrained physical estimates of the
structural shifts induced by observed geometry in the rigid-wall limit
($\alpha_w = \VarAlphaW$). Reintroducing the realistic elastic wall thickness
scaling ($p = \VarP$, yielding $\alpha_w = \VarAlphaWTwo$) shifts these
attractors upward: the symmetric baseline becomes $\alpha^* \approx
\VarAlphaHeteroBaseElastic$, and the combined asymmetric-tapered attractor
reaches $\alpha^* \approx \VarAlphaHeteroCombinedElastic$. The combined
shift (\VarAlphaHeteroCombinedShift{} for rigid,
\VarAlphaHeteroCombinedElasticShift{} for elastic) is not the linear sum of
individual shifts ($\VarAlphaHeteroAsymShift{} + \VarAlphaHeteroTaperShift{} =
-0.010$), reflecting nonlinear coupling in the minimax optimization where
asymmetry and taper effects interact through the wave reflection landscape.

\paragraph{Incoherent vs. Coherent Wave Physics.}
We emphasize that this first-order estimator employs an incoherent power
reflection summation approximation (adding $|\Gamma|^2$ across generations),
which represents a simplified, phase-averaged perturbation model. In contrast,
the complete morphometric model of Paper~II~\cite{paperII} employs a fully
coherent complex impedance transmission line formulation, accounting for
phase-coherent resonances, multiple wave reflections, and generation-specific
parameters. While the simplified incoherent approximation is sufficient to
capture the direction and order of magnitude of the structural perturbations
($\Delta\alpha^* \approx \VarAlphaHeteroCombinedShift$), the rigorous coherent
model of Paper~II is required to fully resolve the anatomical coupling,
converging precisely on the empirical mammalian coronary attractor $\alpha^*
\approx \VarAlphaStar$.

The shift in $\alpha^*$ is governed by the structural perturbations to the wave
reflection landscape. Under physiological conditions, the continuous distal
vessel taper ($\VarHeteroTaperPercent\%$ radius reduction per generation) acts
as a strong regulatory constraint. By introducing a distributed reflection cost
throughout each segment, it pulls the optimal minimax attractor $\alpha^*$
downward. In contrast, bifurcation asymmetry ($A=\VarHeteroAsymmetryA$)
shifts the local wave-reflection attractor upward, while its net effect on
the minimax saddle is small and slightly downward ($\alpha^* \approx
\VarAlphaHeteroAsym$). The net result of their coupling is a combined
equilibrium of $\alpha^* = \VarAlphaHeteroCombined$, showing that vascular
tapering acts as a vital stabilizing mechanism that keeps the network-level
design closer to the wave-optimum, protecting the system from runaway
transport/wave cost amplification.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
These estimates indicate that the structural factors identified in morphometric
datasets represent competing physical forces. Asymmetry, taper, and coherence
act as regulatory constraints that shape the equilibrium. The symmetric model
yields $\alpha^* = \VarAlphaStarModel$, in agreement with intermediate-sized
mammals ($M \sim 100\text{--}500\,$g, e.g., rat pulmonary arteries at $M=300\,$g
with $\alpha \approx 2.68$). Larger mammals ($M \gg 500\,$g) exhibit
systematically higher values ($\alpha^* \approx \VarAlphaStar$ in porcine
coronaries) due to accumulated scale-dependent heterogeneities. The
gauge-invariant analytical model isolates the foundational transition-regime
attractor ($\VarAlphaStarModel$), while the complete quantification of
allometric scaling effects is treated in the full morphometric model of
Paper~II~\cite{paperII}.

The theoretical framework spanning this series of studies originates at the
static optimum derived in Paper~I~\cite{paperI} ($\alpha_t \approx 2.90$). The
minimax model of the present work (Paper~IV) accounts for most of this
transition, shifting the theoretical baseline down to $\alpha^* \approx
\VarAlphaStarModel$. The results of this first-order analysis demonstrate that
physiological asymmetry and taper shift the baseline by approximately
\VarAlphaHeteroCombinedShift{} relative to the symmetric value. The systematic
variation of $\alpha^*$ with body mass above $M^*$ is consistent with the
physical interpretation: the minimax attractor is exact at the transition
threshold and perturbed by scale-dependent morphometric complexity in large
mammals.

\section{Geometric Foundations and the Kinematic Matching Criterion}
\label{sec:S4}

The connection between the 1D pulsatile hydrodynamics of individual vessels and
the $d$-dimensional spatial architecture of the vascular network is established
by the \emph{Kinematic Matching Criterion}
(Theorem~\ref{thm:kinematic_matching}). The condition $\mathcal{Q}^{-1} = d-1$
is derived from three independent physical arguments---Euclidean geometry,
classical kinematics, and evanescent mode absorption---combined with the
Navier-Stokes expression for viscous absorption capacity.

\subsection{Physical Derivation of the Kinematic Matching Criterion}

To establish the criterion, we consider a vascular bifurcation with parent
vessel radius $R$ and daughter vessels embedded in a $d$-dimensional space.

\paragraph{1. Complex Power Balance (Navier-Stokes).}
The fluid dynamics within the bifurcation are governed by the time-harmonic
incompressible Navier-Stokes equations under a pulsatile driving pressure of
frequency $\omega$. By taking the dot product of the momentum equation with the
complex conjugate of the velocity field, $\mathbf{v}^*$, and integrating over
the junction volume $\Omega$, we obtain the multi-dimensional Complex Power
Balance:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:complex_power_balance_supp}
    \Pi_{\mathrm{in}} = \Pi_{\mathrm{out}} + \int_\Omega \hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}} : \nabla\hat{\mathbf{v}}^* \, dV + 2i\omega \int_\Omega \frac{1}{2}\rho |\hat{\mathbf{v}}|^2 \, dV,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\tau}}$ is the viscous stress tensor, $\rho$ is the
fluid density, $\Pi_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the incoming complex power, and
$\Pi_{\mathrm{out}}$ is the outgoing complex power.

\paragraph{2. Viscous Absorption Capacity from Navier-Stokes.}
The exact Womersley solution for oscillatory pipe flow gives the longitudinal
admittance $Y_L$ via Bessel functions. In the transitional regime ($\mathrm{Wo}
\sim 1$), the Womersley function is expanded as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:F10_expansion_supp}
    F_{10}(z) = \frac{2J_1(z)}{zJ_0(z)} \approx 1 + \frac{z^2}{8} + \mathcal{O}(z^4),
    \quad z = e^{-i\pi/4}\,\mathrm{Wo}.
\end{equation}
Substituting $z^2 = -i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2$ into the modified admittance
$M'_{10} = -(8i/\mathrm{Wo}^2)(1 - F_{10})$ yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M10_expansion_supp}
    M'_{10} = 1 - \frac{i\,\mathrm{Wo}^2}{6} + \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Wo}^4).
\end{equation}
Since $Y_L \propto M'_{10}$, the inverse quality factor of the longitudinal
admittance is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tan_phi_asymptotic_supp}
    \mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L} = \frac{\text{Re}(Y_L)}{|\text{Im}(Y_L)|} = \frac{6}{\mathrm{Wo}^2} + \mathcal{O}(\mathrm{Wo}^{0}).
\end{equation}
This is a direct consequence of the Navier-Stokes equations---not a
phenomenological fit---and quantifies the viscous absorption capacity of the
Womersley flow: the energy dissipated per cycle relative to the energy stored in
inertial oscillation.

\paragraph{3. Geometric Scattering at the Junction.}
The spatial transition of the flow direction from the parent longitudinal axis
($\hat{\mathbf{e}}_p$) to the daughter branches ($\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1,
\hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$) requires a transverse kinematic deflection of the fluid.
For a symmetric bifurcation with branching angle $\varphi$ in $d$ dimensions,
the kinematic projection of the velocity vectors implies that the ratio of
transverse-to-axial kinetic energy is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kinetic_ratio_supp}
    \frac{\langle E_{\mathrm{kin}}^{\mathrm{transverse}} \rangle}{\langle E_{\mathrm{kin}}^{\mathrm{axial}} \rangle} = \tan^2\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right).
\end{equation}
To fill the embedding space isotropically and minimize shear stress gradients,
the daughter directions must explore the $(d-1)$ transverse dimensions
uniformly, maximizing the configurational entropy of the branches. This
space-filling symmetry is achieved when the daughters align with the diagonals
of a $d$-dimensional hypercube centered at the junction, yielding:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:isotropic_entropy_supp}
    \cos\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \tan^2\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right) = d - 1.
\end{equation}
Crucially, the transverse velocity component $v_\perp$ cannot propagate in the
daughter vessels, which accept only axial flow. It constitutes an
\emph{evanescent mode} confined to the junction region. By energy conservation,
this evanescent energy must either be absorbed by viscous dissipation or
scattered into a backward-propagating (reflected) wave in the parent vessel.

\paragraph{4. Critical Threshold: the Vascular Ioffe-Regel Criterion.}
The viscous absorption capacity per cycle is $W_{\mathrm{abs}} =
\mathcal{Q}^{-1} \times E_\parallel$ (from the Navier-Stokes result,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:tan_phi_asymptotic_supp}), while the geometric scattering produces
$E_\perp = (d-1) \times E_\parallel$ at each junction
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:kinetic_ratio_supp} with Eq.~\eqref{eq:isotropic_entropy_supp}).
Complete absorption of the evanescent energy (zero reflection, $\Gamma = 0$)
requires $W_{\mathrm{abs}} \geq E_\perp$, i.e., $\mathcal{Q}^{-1} \geq d-1$.
Three regimes emerge:
\begin{itemize}
\item
$\mathcal{Q}^{-1} > d-1$ ($\mathrm{Wo} < \mathrm{Wo}_c$): \emph{Overdamped.}
Viscous absorption exceeds geometric scattering. All transverse energy is
dissipated locally. No coherent wave propagation.
\item
$\mathcal{Q}^{-1} = d-1$ ($\mathrm{Wo} = \mathrm{Wo}_c$): \emph{Critical
threshold.} Absorption exactly balances scattering---the onset of wave
propagation.
\item
$\mathcal{Q}^{-1} < d-1$ ($\mathrm{Wo} > \mathrm{Wo}_c$): \emph{Underdamped.}
Absorption is insufficient; excess transverse energy backscatters as a reflected
wave ($\Gamma \neq 0$) at every junction.
\end{itemize}
The critical condition is therefore:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:self_consistency_supp}
    \mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_L}(\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}}) = d - 1.
\end{equation}
This is the \emph{vascular analogue of the Ioffe-Regel criterion}: the threshold
where geometric scattering overwhelms viscous damping, in heuristic analogy
with the onset of Anderson localization of waves in disordered media (a
qualitative parallel invoked for physical intuition, not a formal
correspondence).

Combining with the Navier-Stokes result
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:tan_phi_asymptotic_supp}):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:woc_derivation_supp}
    \frac{6}{(\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}})^2} = d - 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{\frac{6}{d-1}}.
\end{equation}
For mammalian trees ($d=3$): $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{3} \approx
1.732$. Numerical verification from the full Bessel solution supports the
threshold to within $0.5\%$ ($\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{num}} \approx 1.740$). In a
planar network ($d=2$), the same criterion yields
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{fluid}} = \sqrt{6} \approx \VarWoCTwo$. The
corresponding wave threshold ($\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}}$) follows the Phase
Decoupling derivation detailed in Section~\ref{sec:S1}.

\subsection{Note on the $2\pi$ Factor and Mode Conversion}

We address two technical nuances in the kinematic matching criterion:

\textbf{1. The $2\pi$ Factor in the Quality Factor Definition.} In the standard
analysis of a harmonic oscillator, the inverse quality factor is often defined
in terms of energy per cycle: $Q^{-1} = \Delta E_{\text{cycle}} / (2\pi
E_{\text{stored}})$, which yields $\Delta E_{\text{cycle}} = 2\pi Q^{-1}
E_{\text{stored}}$. However, in our derivation, we define the inverse quality
factor directly from the complex admittance: $\mathcal{Q}^{-1} \equiv
\mathrm{Re}(Y_L)/|\mathrm{Im}(Y_L)|$. This expression measures the ratio of the
time-averaged dissipated power ($P_{\mathrm{diss}}$) to the reactive power
($P_{\mathrm{react}} = \omega E_{\text{stored}}$). Because both the transverse
and longitudinal modes oscillate at the same frequency $\omega$, their power
ratio is identical to their kinetic energy ratio: $P_\perp / P_\parallel =
E_\perp / E_\parallel = d-1$. The energy balance at the junction is evaluated by
equating these mean powers, $P_{\mathrm{diss}} = \mathcal{Q}^{-1} P_\parallel
\ge P_\perp$, which removes the need for any $2\pi$ conversion factors.

\textbf{2. Modal Conversion.} The derivation assumes that the transverse kinetic
energy $E_\perp$ constitutes an evanescent mode that must be either viscously
absorbed or backscattered. In reality, a fraction of this transverse energy may
be converted into the propagating axial mode in the daughter vessels (modal
conversion or refraction) due to the complex flow profiles at the carina. The
condition $\mathcal{Q}^{-1} = d-1$ is therefore a \emph{sufficient} condition
for zero reflection, assuming worst-case complete evanescence. Strikingly, the
numerical verification using the full Bessel solution of the Navier-Stokes
equations---which naturally incorporates all such mode conversion
effects---agrees with the threshold derived from this worst-case boundary
condition to within $0.5\%$. This suggests that modal conversion is small
at the critical Womersley transition, and the analytic threshold remains robust.

\subsection{Thermodynamic Foundations of Cost Extensivity}
The assumption of metabolic cost extensivity (Theorem~\ref{thm:additivity}) is
grounded in the linear stoichiometry of ATP hydrolysis. Both viscous pumping
work and metabolic maintenance represent physical work and thermodynamic state
functions, which are fundamentally extensive. Local selective pressures or
non-linear fitness landscapes (such as threshold-based hypoxia penalties)
represent ecological perturbations on this universal baseline, rather than
destroying the underlying attractor. Linear additivity thus isolates the
thermodynamic ground state of vascular evolution.

\subsection{Geometric Derivation of the Optimal Branching Angle}
We derive the optimal branching angle from a \textbf{maximum entropy principle},
following the approach of Jaynes in statistical mechanics: in the absence of
constraints, nature selects the most symmetric (highest entropy) configuration.

\begin{postulate}[Maximum Configurational Entropy]
\label{post:max_entropy_supp}
For a symmetric bifurcation embedded in $\mathbb{R}^d$, the optimal branching
angle $\varphi$ maximizes the configurational entropy of the daughter
directions, subject to the constraint of isotropic space-filling.
\end{postulate}

Consider a parent vessel along $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_p$ splitting into two daughters
$\hat{\mathbf{e}}_1, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_2$ with angle $\varphi$ between them. For
the network to fill $d$-dimensional space isotropically, the daughters must
explore the $(d-1)$ transverse dimensions uniformly.

\textbf{Geometric constraint:} By symmetry, each daughter must project equally
onto all $(d-1)$ orthogonal directions. In $\mathbb{R}^d$, this is achieved when
the daughters align with the diagonals of a $d$-dimensional hypercube centered
at the parent. For such alignment, the projection of each daughter onto the
parent axis is:
\begin{equation}
    \cos\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}.
    \label{eq:geometric_constraint_supp}
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[Justification via Hypercube Geometry]
In a $d$-dimensional hypercube with edge length $a$, the diagonal connecting
opposite vertices has length $a\sqrt{d}$. The projection of this diagonal onto
any single axis is $a$. Thus, the angle between the diagonal and any axis is
$\cos^{-1}(1/\sqrt{d})$. For a bifurcation to explore all $d$ dimensions
symmetrically, the daughter vessels must align with these diagonals.
\end{remark}

From Eq.~\eqref{eq:geometric_constraint_supp}:
\begin{equation}
    \sin^2\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right) = 1 - \frac{1}{d} = \frac{d-1}{d} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boxed{\tan^2\left(\frac{\varphi}{2}\right) = d - 1.}
    \label{eq:angle_formula_supp}
\end{equation}

For $d=3$: $\tan(\varphi^*/2) = \sqrt{2}$ $\Rightarrow$ $\varphi^* =
\VarAngleTetrahedralDeg^\circ$ (tetrahedral angle).

\subsection{Physical Interpretation of the Critical Womersley Threshold}
The critical Womersley number $\mathrm{Wo}_c$ is the dynamic threshold governing
the transition between viscous-dominated (Poiseuille) and inertia-dominated
(Womersley) flow. For a pulsatile flow at frequency $\omega$ in a vessel of
radius $R$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu$, the viscous penetration depth is
$\delta = \sqrt{\nu/\omega}$. The Womersley number is the ratio $\mathrm{Wo} =
R/\delta$.

When $\delta \approx R$ ($\mathrm{Wo} \approx 1$), the viscous boundary layer
occupies the entire vessel, and the velocity profile is nearly parabolic.

\subsection{Analytical Limits}
The complex vascular admittance $Y(\mathrm{Wo})$ governs the pulsatile
pressure-flow relation. For a vessel of radius $r$, $Y = (\pi r^2 [1-F_{10}]) /
(i\omega\rho)$. The condition for a reflectionless junction ($\Gamma = 0$) in a
symmetric binary tree is $Y_p = 2 Y_c$. We analyze the two physical extremes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item
\textbf{High-Womersley Limit ($\mathrm{Wo} \gg 1$):} The viscous boundary layer
is negligible. The Womersley function $F_{10} \to 0$. The admittance simplifies
to $Y \propto r^2$. The matching condition $r_p^2 = 2 r_c^2$ requires $\alpha =
2$.
    \item
\textbf{Low-Womersley Limit ($\mathrm{Wo} \ll 1$):} The flow is dominated by
viscosity. In this regime, the Poiseuille resistance $R_{\mathrm{visc}} = 8\mu
L/(\pi r^4)$ governs the flow. For impedance matching at a bifurcation, the
condition $R_{\mathrm{visc},p} = R_{\mathrm{visc},c1} \parallel
R_{\mathrm{visc},c2}$ (parallel combination) yields $r_p^4 = 2 r_c^4$ for
symmetric branches. However, Murray's Law ($\alpha = 3$) emerges from minimizing
the combined cost of viscous dissipation plus metabolic maintenance, giving the
optimal condition $r_p^3 = 2 r_c^3$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Viscous Shielding and Pulse Attenuation}
The propagation of the pulsatile energy along a vessel of length $L$ is subject
to viscous decay. The complex wavenumber $k$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    k(\mathrm{Wo}) = \frac{\omega}{c_0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - F_{10}(\mathrm{Wo})}}.
\end{equation}
The power attenuation factor $\eta_{\mathrm{att}} = e^{-2 \kappa L}$, where
$\kappa = \mathrm{Re}[-i k] = \mathrm{Im}(k)$. For a typical conduit vessel
($L/R \approx 25$), the attenuation is negligible for $\mathrm{Wo} > 5$ but
becomes dominant as $\mathrm{Wo} \to 1$, naturally 'shielding' the
microcirculation from pulsatile energy. This mechanism eliminates the need for
heuristic damping factors in the wave cost functional.

\subsection{Robustness and Stress-Test Defenses of the Kinematic Matching Criterion}
To establish the mathematical and physical resilience of the Kinematic Matching
Criterion, we address four critical physical ``stress-test'' criticisms
regarding local fluid dynamics at the bifurcation:

\paragraph{Defense 1: Convergence Radius of the Low-Womersley Expansion.}
A potential criticism is that the second-order asymptotic expansion $\tan\phi
\approx 6/\mathrm{Wo}^2$ diverges as $\mathrm{Wo}$ increases beyond the
transitional regime. However, at the critical threshold $\mathrm{Wo}_c =
\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$, the expansion remains highly accurate. Evaluating the
full Bessel-ratio solution $\phi(\mathrm{Wo})$ numerically without
approximations yields $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\text{num}} \approx 1.740$. The relative
error of the analytical truncation is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Error} = \frac{|1.732 - 1.740|}{1.740} \approx 0.46\%,
\end{equation}
which is well below $0.5\%$. This shows that the analytical ground state
represents an exceptionally strong and stable physical attractor in the
transitional regime, where higher-order terms contribute negligible
perturbations.

\paragraph{Defense 2: Local Navier-Stokes Non-linearities and Boundary Layer Separation.}
At the carina (apex) of a 3D bifurcation, the 1D Womersley flow breaks down,
generating localized non-linearities, boundary layer separation, and secondary
Dean-like vortices. One might argue that these multi-dimensional effects
invalidate the 1D admittance expansion. However, the Complex Power Balance
\eqref{eq:complex_power_balance_supp} is a global volume-integral relation. By
integrating over the junction volume $\Omega$, localized velocity singularities
and non-linear convective terms are naturally averaged and absorbed. Since the
local Reynolds number in Capillary/Arteriolar transition zones is small ($Re
\approx 100$), the flow remains globally laminar, and the integrated complex
power is dominated by the linear viscous and inertial terms, validating the
stability of the global kinematic matching threshold.

\paragraph{Defense 3: Axial Leakage of Reactive Power into Daughter Vessels.}
A third stress-test concerns whether a portion of the reactive phase power
propagates (leaks) past the bifurcation into the daughter vessels, violating the
local self-consistency balance. Under standard physiological conditions, the
metabolic gauge optimization (Theorem~\ref{thm:gauge}) operates precisely to
minimize wave reflections ($\Gamma \to 0$) and impedance mismatching at the
junctions. Since the minimax attractor enforces near-perfect impedance matching,
the transmission of reactive power is highly regulated, and the junction
operates as a self-contained, reflectionless transformer, suppressing axial
leakage and locking the kinematic matching condition at the carina.

\paragraph{Defense 4: Wall Compliance and Moens-Korteweg Wall Elasticity.}
Finally, one might argue that wall elasticity (compliance) shifts the fluid
admittance by introducing a capacitive component. However, the vascular network
exhibits a property of \emph{Topological Shielding} under hierarchical scaling.
In the large conduit arteries where wall compliance is significant, the
Womersley number is high ($\mathrm{Wo} \gg \sqrt{3}$), placing the system far
above the transition threshold where diameter scaling is dominated by the
wave-optimum ($\alpha \approx 2$). As vessels scale down toward the transition
zone ($\mathrm{Wo} \sim \sqrt{3}$), the absolute wall thickness-to-radius ratio
increases, and the surrounding tissue matrix provides rigid reinforcement. This
suppresses wall compliance in the transition regime, decoupling the critical
Womersley threshold from viscoelastic wall parameters and shielding the
geometric transition.

\subsection{Numerical Verification of the Critical Threshold}

To verify the accuracy of the low-Womersley analytical prediction, we
numerically evaluate the exact Bessel-ratio solution for $\phi(\mathrm{Wo})$
without approximations. The reactive phase lag is computed directly from the
definition:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(\mathrm{Wo}) = \arg \left[ \frac{1}{1 - F_{10}(\mathrm{Wo})} \right], \quad F_{10}(\mathrm{Wo}) = \frac{2 J_1(\mathrm{Wo} e^{-i\pi/4})}{\mathrm{Wo} e^{-i\pi/4} J_0(\mathrm{Wo} e^{-i\pi/4})}.
\end{equation}

The numerical root of $\tan \phi(\mathrm{Wo}) = 2$ is found to be
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\text{num}} \approx 1.740$, comparing favorably with the
analytical prediction of $\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732$ (relative error $< 0.5\%$; see
Figure~\ref{fig:womersley_verification}). We emphasize that while $\sqrt{3}$ is
the exact attractor derived from the $SO(3)$ rotational symmetry of the 3D
spatial embedding, the value $1.740$ represents its realization within the
specific viscous profile of a Newtonian fluid in a rigid pipe. The proximity of
these values support the interpretation that the incommensurability
transition is anchored by the fundamental spatial dimension rather than by
specific fluid-dynamic contingencies.

The same exact-Bessel procedure applied to the \emph{characteristic}
admittance checks the wave threshold quoted in the main text. Taking $Y_c
\propto \sqrt{1-F_{10}}$ (the Womersley--McDonald characteristic admittance) and
solving $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_c}(\mathrm{Wo}) = d-1$ numerically yields
$\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}} \approx 2.144$ for $d=3$, agreeing with the
analytical $3/\sqrt{2}\approx 2.121$ to within $1\%$---the same accuracy as the
fluid threshold. For $d=2$ one finds $\mathcal{Q}^{-1}_{Y_c}\ge 1$ for every
$\mathrm{Wo}>0$ (increasing with $\mathrm{Wo}$), so the wave threshold collapses
to $\mathrm{Wo}_c^{\mathrm{wave}}(d{=}2)=0$, showing the permanent wave
dominance that underlies the retinal analysis (\S\ref{sec:retinal}). The naive
identification $Y_c\propto\sqrt{Y_L}$, which omits the $e^{i\pi/4}$ phase of
$\sqrt{i\omega\rho}$, would instead give $\approx 2.93$ and is therefore
excluded. The wave threshold is thus numerically checked against the exact
Bessel solution, not merely posited.

The Kinematic Matching Criterion relates $\mathrm{Wo}_c = \sqrt{3}$ to an
architectural eigenvalue, marking the point where the fluid dynamical parameters
(viscous penetration) match the geometric constraints of the 3D embedding.

This relation connects:

\textbf{Geometric optimization:} Optimal branching angle from maximum entropy,
    \item
\textbf{Dynamical optimization:} Critical Womersley number from viscous
penetration and energy equipartition,
    \item
\textbf{Dimensional constraint:} Embedding dimension $d$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Cardiac Pulse Spectrum and Wave Power Integration}

The pulsatile energy of the heart is not concentrated in a single frequency but
is distributed across a discrete spectrum of harmonics $\{n\omega_H\}$. To
define a pre-specified formulation, we define the wave cost functional
$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wave}}$ as the spectral sum of reflected power:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wave}}(\alpha) = \sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{max}}} H_n \cdot \mathcal{R}(\alpha, n\omega_H),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}$ is the network-summed reflection loss and $H_n =
(c_n/c_0)^2$ is the relative power of the $n$-th harmonic. For a standard
mammalian aortic flow wave (approximated as a half-sine of duty cycle $1/3$
followed by $2/3$ diastole), the Fourier coefficients $c_n$ yield the fixed
physical weights: $H_1 \approx 1.12$, $H_2 \approx 0.65$, $H_3 \approx 0.28$,
with successive harmonics ($n \geq 4$) carrying progressively smaller
weights.

Sensitivity analysis shows that $\alpha^*$ is remarkably robust to the specific
choice of the pulse waveform. Variations of $\pm 20\%$ in the harmonic weights
$H_n$ result in a shift of $\Delta \alpha^* < 0.02$. This stability arises
because the fundamental harmonic $H_1$ contains over 50\% of the oscillatory
power and effectively determines the location of the minimax saddle point.

\section{Limits of the Thin-Wall Approximation in the Terminal Regime}
\label{sec:S5}

This section asks whether the thin-wall assumption behind the wave
attractor could break down in the tiniest vessels, where the wall is no longer
thin compared with the lumen. The short answer, made quantitative below, is that
it does not matter for the architecture: the thick-wall correction grows
precisely where the Womersley number becomes small, so the wave channel---and
with it any sensitivity to the wall model---is already switched off by viscous
dominance.

The generalized impedance attractor $\alpha_w = (5-p)/2$ derived in the
main text assumes the thin-wall approximation ($h \ll r$), where the incremental
elastic modulus $E$ and wall thickness $h$ relate to the Moens-Korteweg wave
speed $c = \sqrt{Eh/2\rho r}$.

In the terminal arterioles, where $h/r \approx \VarThicknessRatioArterioles$,
the thick-wall correction factor for the wave speed follows from the Lam\'{e}
elastic solution for a pressurised thick cylinder:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{c_{\mathrm{thick}}}{c_{\mathrm{thin}}}
    = \sqrt{1 + \frac{h}{2r}}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the lumen radius and $h$ is the wall thickness. This expression
reduces to unity for $h \to 0$ (thin-wall limit) and yields a $\approx 10\%$
correction at $h/r = \VarThicknessRatioArterioles$. For the aorta ($h/r \approx
\VarThicknessRatioAorta$), the correction is $\approx
\VarLameCorrectionPercent\%$, showing that the thin-wall approximation is
rigorous in the proximal conduit vessels.

\textbf{Topological Shielding Principle.} The architectural impact of the
thick-wall correction in distal generations is asymptotically suppressed because
the Womersley viscous-suppression factor $S(\mathrm{Wo}) =
\mathrm{Wo}^4/(\mathrm{Wo}^4 + \mathrm{Wo}_c^4)$ vanishes as $\mathrm{Wo} \to
0$. Even a $\sim 10\%$ correction to the wave speed in the terminal arterioles
leaves the reflection coefficient $|\Gamma_g|^2 \propto S(\mathrm{Wo}_g) \to 0$
unchanged in order of magnitude. The global network cost is therefore dominated
by the proximal conduit generations where $\mathrm{Wo} \gg \mathrm{Wo}_c$ and
the thin-wall approximation remains rigorous. In the extreme terminal arteriole
regime ($\mathrm{Wo} \to 0$), where traditional linear wave speed models break
down and dynamic metabolic requirements dominate, active neurovascular coupling
and smooth muscle regulation dynamically alter compliance and vascular diameters
to bypass static geometric bounds~\cite{uhlirova2017}.

\subsection{Unified Perspective}

The original three arguments (SO($d$) symmetry, maximum entropy, variational
embedding) are \emph{not} independent justifications, but \emph{three
perspectives} on the same variational free energy principle:

\begin{itemize}
    \item
\textbf{SO($d$) symmetry:} Ensures $U_{\mathrm{geom}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{config}}$
depend only on scalar invariants,
    \item
\textbf{Maximum entropy:} Derives $S_{\mathrm{config}}$ from configuration space
volume $\Omega(\varphi)$,
    \item
\textbf{Variational embedding:} Minimizes $F = U - TS$ to find $\varphi^*$.
\end{itemize}

All three converge to the same formula $\tan^2(\varphi^*/2) = d - 1$,
demonstrating robustness of the result. For three-dimensional embedding ($d=3$),
the critical Womersley number satisfies $\mathrm{Wo}_c^2 = 6/(d-1) = 3$ (i.e.,
$\mathrm{Wo}_c = \sqrt{3}$), unifying geometric and dynamical optimization.
The compact form $\mathrm{Wo}_c^2 = d$ is a coincidence special to $d=3$

\section{Mathematical Foundations of Optimization and Invariance}
\label{sec:S7}

This section provides the rigorous mathematical proofs and extended derivations
supporting the theorems presented in the main text. Specifically, we detail the
uniqueness of the linear metabolic penalty through the lens of functional
equations, and establish the thermodynamic bounds of the near-equilibrium
regime.

\subsection{Formal Proof of Segmentation Invariance}
\label{sec:S7_segmentation}

We prove that the linear metabolic penalty is the unique functional form that
preserves \emph{Compositional Invariance} (or \emph{Segmentation Invariance})
across arbitrary sub-networks of the vascular tree.

\subsubsection{Compositional Consistency and the Jensen Functional Equation}

Let the vascular network be partitioned into $n$ arbitrary sub-networks. The net
active metabolic penalty of the network can be assessed either globally or as a
weighted sum of the segment-level penalties. Let $\Phi_{i,\mathrm{opt}}$ be the
optimal baseline metabolic dissipation of the $i$-th sub-network, and
$\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \Phi_{i,\mathrm{opt}}$ be the global
baseline metabolic dissipation. The baseline weights are defined as $w_i =
\Phi_{i,\mathrm{opt}} / \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$, satisfying $\sum_{i=1}^n w_i = 1$.

Let $F(x)$ be a continuous, dimensionless fitness penalty function defined on
the relative dissipation ratio $x = \Phi / \Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$. If the network
is assessed globally, the net penalty is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{global}} = F\left( \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \Phi_i}{\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}} \right) = F\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_j \right),
\end{equation}
where $x_j = \Phi_j / \Phi_{j,\mathrm{opt}}$ represents the local dissipation
ratio of the $j$-th sub-network.

Conversely, if the penalty is assessed via segmentation (sub-network
decomposition), the composite penalty is the weighted average of the individual
segment penalties:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{segmented}} = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i F(x_i).
\end{equation}

For the physical optimization state ($\alpha^*$) to be an objective property of
the network independent of the observer's chosen decomposition resolution (i.e.,
independent of $n$ and the partition boundaries), we must have:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{global}} = \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{segmented}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad F\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i \right) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i F(x_i),
    \label{eq:jensen_weighted_supp}
\end{equation}
which must hold for any arbitrary partitioning $w_i$ and independent local
states $x_i$.

\subsubsection{Uniqueness of the Affine Form}

Equation~\eqref{eq:jensen_weighted_supp} is the generalized \emph{weighted
Jensen (affinity) equation} (or the Cauchy weighted-average equation) for
continuous functions. Assuming $F$ is differentiable, we can determine the
general solution by taking the partial derivative with respect to a local state
$x_j$:
\begin{equation}
    w_j F'\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i \right) = w_j F'(x_j).
\end{equation}
Dividing both sides by the arbitrary weight $w_j \neq 0$ yields:
\begin{equation}
    F'\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i \right) = F'(x_j).
\end{equation}
Because the left-hand side depends on all states $\{x_i\}$, whereas the
right-hand side is purely a local function of $x_j$, this equality can hold if
and only if the derivative of the penalty function is a global constant:
\begin{equation}
    F'(x) = c \quad \forall x.
\end{equation}
Integrating both sides, we obtain the strictly affine form:
\begin{equation}
    F(x) = c(x - 1) + F(1).
\end{equation}
By setting the penalty at the optimum to zero ($F(1) = 0$) and absorbing the
positive scaling constant $c$ into the global selection coefficient, we uniquely
recover the linear metabolic gauge:
\begin{equation}
    F(x) = x - 1.
\end{equation}
Any non-linear penalty function (e.g., quadratic $F(x) = (x-1)^2$ or logarithmic
$F(x) = \ln x$) strictly violates the Jensen functional relation. Consequently,
non-linear penalties would couple the global optimization path to the specific
number of segments $n$ and the boundaries of the spatial mesh, introducing an
unphysical resolution-dependence and breaking the self-consistency of the
evolutionary attractor.


\subsection{Systematic Exclusion of Alternative Cost Functionals}
\label{sec:S7_alternatives}

The linear penalty $F(x)=x-1$ is not posited but selected: it is the
unique functional surviving four independent admissibility requirements, each
rooted in a distinct physical principle. To make the exclusion of alternatives
explicit---rather than leaving it implicit in the uniqueness proof above---we
test the most natural candidate forms against these requirements:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item
\textbf{Scale invariance (metabolic gauge).} $F$ may depend only on the
dimensionless ratio $x=\Phi/\Phi_{\mathrm{opt}}$, with $F(1)=0$; any dependence
on absolute scales would require a biologically privileged reference mass, which
is absent across the $10^{5}$-fold allometric range.
  \item
\textbf{Compositional (resolution) consistency.} Global and segment-wise
assessment must agree for an arbitrary partition, forcing $F$ to satisfy the
weighted Jensen (affinity) equation \eqref{eq:jensen_weighted_supp}.
  \item
\textbf{Onsager linear response.} Near the optimum the penalty must be linear in
the excess dissipation $\Delta\Phi=\Phi-\Phi^{*}$, as required by the linearity
of irreversible thermodynamics close to a stationary state.
  \item
\textbf{Non-substitutability.} The aggregation of the two incommensurable cost
modes must forbid mutual compensation: unbounded wave loss cannot be offset by
efficient transport.
\end{enumerate}

with additive minimax aggregation satisfies all four; every alternative fails at
least one, and the failures are qualitative---broken self-consistency or
admissibility---not matters of empirical fit.

and resolution-consistent, but it renders the two failure modes fungible, which
is biologically inadmissible for independently vital functions. The linear
excess is therefore the unique cost functional consistent with the physics of
the problem. This both strengthens and delimits the central claim: linearity is
\emph{forced} by these four requirements, and would be relaxed only if one of
them were physically rejected---a possibility we regard as the most promising
target for future falsification.


\subsection{Convexity of the Wave-Reflection Functional}
\label{sec:S7_convexity}

The existence and uniqueness of the minimax saddle point $\alpha^*$ rely on the
strict convexity of the wave-reflection functional
$\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wave}}(\alpha)$. We present an analytic argument
valid in a neighbourhood of the minimum, and then verify convexity over the full
physiological range numerically; we do not claim a closed-form global proof.

\subsubsection{Local Convexity Argument and Numerical Verification}

We show analytically that the functional is convex near its minimizer, and
verify numerically that it remains strictly convex throughout the physiological
range $\alpha \in [2, 3]$ for every characteristic Womersley number
$\mathrm{Wo}$ tested.

\paragraph{Local argument (near the minimizer).}
Let $\zeta(\alpha) = |\Gamma(\alpha)|^2 = \left| \frac{u(\alpha)\chi -
1}{u(\alpha)\chi + 1} \right|^2$ be the reflection penalty at a junction, where
$u(\alpha) = N^{2/\alpha-1}$ and $\chi(\mathrm{Wo})$ is the ratio of complex
admittances (normalized for viscous effects).

The second derivative $d^2\zeta/d\alpha^2 = \zeta''(u) (u')^2 + \zeta'(u) u''$
determines the curvature. Near the minimum ($\alpha \approx \alpha_w$),
$\zeta'(u) = O(u-1)$ ensures that the cross term is of higher order, so
$\zeta''>0$ locally. This argument is local; global strict convexity over
$[2,3]$ is established by the numerical Hessian scan below, which shows
that $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{wave}}''(\alpha) > 0$ everywhere on the tested domain
$\mathrm{Wo} \in [0.1, 20]$.
